This replication project helped me understand how Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA) can inform real-world environmental decision-making. As a policy student, I find QCA particularly valuable because environmental problems rarely result from a single factor. Instead, they come from complex interactions across scientific, economic, social, and political dimensions. QCA allows us to examine how multiple conditions jointly shape policy outcomes, which is essential for designing feasible and context-sensitive environmental strategies.
Replicating the truth table made me realize that policy instruments such as Local Content Requirements (LCRs) do not inherently lead to successful renewable energy industrialization. Effectiveness depends on specific combinations of conditions such as strong renewable energy targets, favorable market environments, or high economic complexity. This perspective encourages policymakers to move beyond one solution and instead consider how policy mixes interact within different national contexts.
The replication also revealed several limitations. Fuzzy-set calibration involves subjective choices that may bias results. The dataset focuses on selected countries, which may not fully represent challenges faced by many developing economies. In addition, the lack of open code in the original study made reproduction more difficult.
Overall, this exercise showed me that QCA is not just a methodological tool but a way of thinking. It helps decision-makers navigate complexity and design more effective, tailored environmental policies.
